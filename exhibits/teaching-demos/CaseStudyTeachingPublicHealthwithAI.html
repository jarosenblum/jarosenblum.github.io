‌Example Case: 

Ms. Rodriguez is a dedicated public health teacher at San Sample High, a school with limited technology resources, but with tremendous faculty resources and a vibrant student body. A formerly professional nurse and public health expert, she wants to provide her students with the self-efficacy they need to successfully navigate public health issues post-pandemic, and in an increasingly challenging public health landscape. She's heard a lot about LLMs and tools like ChatGPT and has experimented with it to generate lesson ideas for her 11th grade. She has even heard about Notebook LM by professors and teachers at San Jose State University. However, she feels lost when trying to understand what "AI" actually is and how it applies to her classroom, or how to best help students apply AI to .

For Teachers: Understanding GenAI Basics

In the context of tools like ChatGPT or NotebookLM, "AI" refers to Artificial Intelligence, specifically a branch called Machine Learning. These tools are trained on vast amounts of data to recognize patterns and generate responses. Use of Generative AI, or GenAI tools, relies upon on the use of standalone LLMs or LLMs embedded in other tools like NotebookLM that have been trained on complex models of language, and that have been trained using trillions of bits of data from information stored primarily on the accessible internet.

  • LLM: LLM is short for “Large Language Model”, and like other types of AI models is a complex mathematical representation of the patterns used to train the LLM. These “models” can then generate text, predict words, or perform other tasks.
  • ChatGPT: ChatGPT is a type of large language model that is commonly used to retrieve information through simple and complex queries. Google’s Claude and Microsoft’s CoPilot are other examples of LLMs.
  • NotebookLM: NotebookLM offers an advanced integration of ChatGPT, allowing students to learn using a “podcast” format that mimics a discussion between two people and referencing any number of sources of information. Notably, NotebookLM contains only the data that a student or teacher provides.
  • Training: Tools like NotebookLM and ChatGPT are “trained” and “fine tuned” through complex machine learning processes that incorporate systems of weighted data, vectors and rules that are used to ingest ‘chunks’ of data offered through a tool like ChatGPT.
  • Data: The quality and quantity of data used to train these models greatly influence their performance. Biased or limited data can lead to biased or inaccurate outputs. Biases can be made through incorrect assumptions about language, by omission of information (knowing or otherwise), and by direct manipulation of the math behind the model.
  • Knowledge: Data does not equal knowledge. Knowledge is formed by helping people to retrieve the right data, with the right contexts that support intended learning outcomes. To quote a certain Italian philosopher (Giambattista Vico, 17th century), the assumption is that, “to know is to know how to make”. In this case, to know how to use AI tools for learning is to know how to help students make meaning from their use of these tools.
  • Generative AI Literacy: Or GenAI literacy, is the broader field of study and discourse around questions about how AI works, what we should know about it to use it, how we should approach teaching AI concepts, and how we should approach applying AI concepts in our work and the work of our students.

Teachers: The Importance of AI Literacy

AI literacy is crucial for teachers like Ms. Rodriguez. It empowers them to:

  • Understand how AI tools work and their potential impact.
  • Critically evaluate AI-generated content and outputs.
  • Integrate AI tools effectively and ethically into teaching practices.
  • Prepare students for a future where AI is increasingly prevalent.

For Students: How to use AI tools to maximize learning.

A strategy to improve student self-efficacy with learning complex topics (like public health) is to help students to learn in teams and by discovery, and in service of solving a problem that connects to their experiences. Here’s an example problem that Ms. Rodriguez can use to challenge her students to use LLMs critically to learn about public health by questioning and interrogating pre-selected sources. Ms. Rodriguez could help her students by creating a study guide for their work in which she helps them to ask:

  • How do I ask: How do I need to phrase my questions to an AI tool? How many questions should I ask and how should I ask them?
  • How do I know: …that the response is accurate, complete and valid?
  • How do I apply: …what I’m learning and use it to accomplish the goals for my lesson?
  • How do I change: …change my approach if I get stuck?

By gaining a basic understanding of GenAI literacy concepts and addressing common critical thinking strategies, Ms. Rodriguez can confidently explore and adopt AI tools to enhance her teaching and student learning about complex public health topics such as SARS CoV-2, implications for current disease outbreaks and strategies for health communication. These GenAI literacy lesson plan components are designed to work within many types of lessons and as such are meant to be integrated into different lesson plans. This lesson plan is designed to be adapted to work with ChatGPT

This GenAI Literacy Lesson example assumes that: 

  1. Students will have prior experience with using ChatGPT
  2. Students will have NotebookLM to locate and interrogate sources of information to learn about the spread of the disease
  3. For Class 2: Ms. Rodriguez will pre-populate a NotebookLM app with pre-selected sources, including facts and public health coverage about SARS CoV-2 and its current spread in CA.
  4. Ms. Rodriguez will initially provide students with access to ChatGPT during the first class, and will enable the students to have access to the podcast discussion for the second class, to support their continued study of the topic.
  5. Students will be challenged with this learning outcome: Following your initial use of a chatbot, use your AI Notebook to review your learning. and write a public health analysis about the benefits of vaccination programs, the role of technologies like social media and AI to learn about the disease, and the risks to vaccinated and unvaccinated communities who are exposed to information-poor sources of information about the disease.

GenAI Literacy Lesson Components: ChatGPT for SARS CoV-2 and Public Health in Northern California.

Lesson time: 45 minutes

Lesson Components

Description

Example

Part 1 AI Literacy (45 min): ask ChatGPT questions

  1. How do I ask questions

Time: 15 minutes

Asking the right questions is a form of “prompt engineering” to an AI tool. LLMs need to know your context + specific instructions + its intended role in answering questions. Students should be provided with a scaffold, in the form of a prompt stem, to help them engineer their prompts for maximal learning. 

Ask students to initially use only ChatGPT for the first class. Provide student teams with a prompt stem and customize it. Then ask them to use it in a LLM  prompt: You are a health communicator who works for the California Dept of Public Health. Provide detailed information about SARS CoV-2 outbreaks in California. Discuss news sources that mention xxxxx and describe the impact of covid on the population. Always include citations. Describe the limitations of your response.

  1. How do I know: Valid Response

Time: 15 minutes

Checking LLM responses means validating those responses. This occurs through a) checking the relevance of the answer against the question, b) interrogating the sources provided and c) interrogating the depth, complexity and logic of the response.

Ask students to swap their responses with another team. Ask the teams to validate each others responses by rating them on a scale of 0-10 across each of the dimensions of: a) relevance, b) sources and c) depth/complexity/logic of the response. Ask teams to provide a 1 sentence explanation for each dimension.

  1. How do I apply my knowledge

Time: 15 minutes

Information learned from an LLM becomes knowledge if it is applied in some way, and if meaning is made from learning. Building knowledge and self-sufficiency in continued exploration and learning leads to self-efficacy. Students should be encouraged to reflect upon how they can apply their learning within their overall lesson.

Ask students to spend 5 min to write a reflection that summarizes:

  • What they learned from their chatbot and how well it did and did not work
  • How they will apply what they learned to answer a question from the broader lesson.
  • What one question do they have now about this tool or the topic?

Part 2: AI Creation + Collective Knowledge Building (50 min): Expand the student view to include Notebook LM’s podcast tool, providing full access to the tool.

  1. How do I learn more? 

Time: 50 minutes

  • may be started in first class if time permits and completed in a subsequent class
  • Students must use their google accounts to login to NotebookLM and create their notebooklm’s for this exercise.

5 min: Once students have reflected, provide them with access to Notebook LM content. Demonstrate the podcast by playing a portion of the podcast and interacting by asking NotebookLM a question.

10 min: Once students are engaged, assign student teams to work together to create a NotebookLM using files from Canvas and to ready the chat tool.

20 min: Let students work in teams to brainstorm more questions to ask NotebookLM, and ask that they take notes using the tool. Teacher assesses the results of group research

15 min: Ask students to reflect

This step helps to support transfer of learning, with a reduced reliance on the original scaffolded prompt, prompt formation of new ideas and record their observations in the notetaking tool.  

Ask students to work in larger teams to ask new questions and to collectively take notes. Students should be encouraged through peer and instructor feedback to ask deeper and more nuanced questions.

As students take notes, they will build upon each other’s work. As questions or notes inevitably overlap between groups, challenge the class to hold a discussion about the content, and prioritize the most important points about the topic.

Ask students to reflect upon their learning and cooperation at the end of this exercise.


AI Integration Challenges for Ms. Rodriguez

  • Ms. Rodriguez will need to devote class time to ask students to login to Notebook LM, or ask that they login to this tool prior to the second class. Students will also need to download Ms. Rodriguez’s sources from Canvas and upload them to NotebookLM.
  • Students will need to learn how to upload files to NotebookLM, and how to create a simple podcast for notetaking. This process will require some time, but students can work in larger groups to offset potential technical issues.
  • Students may be tempted to simply ask the LLM random questions, and they may be tempted to accept initial answers as acceptable, or “good enough”.
  • Ms. Rodriguez will likely need to “model” the example process by demonstrating how she prepared her materials. She might benefit from showing example prompts, before providing the first question stem. However, completing this step will help support the technology-pedagogical knowledge (TPK) and technology-content knowledge (TCK) needed to teach this lesson.
  • Step 4 asks that Ms. Rodriguez enable open access to NotebookLM.
  • Ms. Rodriguez might be teaching in a no-mobile-phone school district, and access to computing devices might be limited. More students might need to share a device.
  • Ms. Rodriguez might benefit from asking students to turn in their prompts as part of assessing the assignment. This step would require that students use the copy function in the LLM chat to copy/paste their work into a googledoc.
