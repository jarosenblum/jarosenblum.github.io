Teachers are exposed to AI tools for learning through popular media and technologies like ChatGPT. However, they also encounter tools through schools by interacting with children, resource specialists and even educational technology vendors. How might teachers evaluate popular tools for inclusion as tools (such as ChatGPT by OpenAI, or NotebookLM by Google) for teaching and learning? This lesson offers teachers the opportunity to:

  • Gain a basic foundation for discussing AI tools and their affordances for learning.
  • Discuss digital equity issues…
  • Gain a basic foundation for discussing literacy about AI tools and their implications for student self-efficacy / equity with learning.
  • Learn how an emerging application, NotebookLM, made by Google can be used to organize and augment classroom instruction and support guided student discovery of a science topic
  • Create a simple NotebookLM application

Supplemental Background

For Teachers: Understanding GenAI Basics

In the context of tools like ChatGPT or NotebookLM, "AI" refers to Artificial Intelligence, specifically a branch called Machine Learning. These tools are trained on vast amounts of data to recognize patterns and generate responses. Use of Generative AI, or GenAI tools, relies upon on the use of standalone LLMs or LLMs embedded in other tools like NotebookLM that have been trained on complex models of language, and that have been trained using trillions of bits of data from information stored primarily on the accessible internet.

  • LLM: LLM is short for “Large Language Model”, and like other types of AI models is a complex mathematical representation of the patterns used to train the LLM. These “models” can then generate text, predict words, or perform other tasks.
  • ChatGPT: ChatGPT is a type of large language model that is commonly used to retrieve information through simple and complex queries. Google’s Claude and Microsoft’s CoPilot are other examples of LLMs.
  • NotebookLM: NotebookLM offers an advanced integration of ChatGPT, allowing students to learn using a “podcast” format that mimics a discussion between two people and referencing any number of sources of information. Notably, NotebookLM contains only the data that a student or teacher provides.
  • Training: Tools like NotebookLM and ChatGPT are “trained” and “fine tuned” through complex machine learning processes that incorporate systems of weighted data, vectors and rules that are used to ingest ‘chunks’ of data offered through a tool like ChatGPT.
  • Data: The quality and quantity of data used to train these models greatly influence their performance. Biased or limited data can lead to biased or inaccurate outputs. Biases can be made through incorrect assumptions about language, by omission of information (knowing or otherwise), and by direct manipulation of the math behind the model.
  • Knowledge: Data does not equal knowledge. Knowledge is formed by helping people to retrieve the right data, with the right contexts that support intended learning outcomes. To quote a certain Italian philosopher (Giambattista Vico, 17th century), the assumption is that, “to know is to know how to make”. In this case, to know how to use AI tools for learning is to know how to help students make meaning from their use of these tools.
  • Generative AI Literacy: Or GenAI literacy, is the broader field of study and discourse around questions about how AI works, what we should know about it to use it, how we should approach teaching AI concepts, and how we should approach applying AI concepts in our work and the work of our students.

Teachers: The Importance of AI Literacy

AI literacy is crucial for teachers like Ms. Rodriguez. It empowers them to:

  • Understand how AI tools work and their potential impact.
  • Critically evaluate AI-generated content and outputs.
  • Integrate AI tools effectively and ethically into teaching practices.
  • Prepare students for a future where AI is increasingly prevalent.

For Students: How to use AI tools to maximize learning.

A strategy to improve student self-efficacy with learning complex topics (like public health) is to help students to learn in teams and by discovery, and in service of solving a problem that connects to their experiences. Here’s an example problem that Ms. Rodriguez can use to challenge her students to use LLMs critically to learn about public health by questioning and interrogating pre-selected sources. Ms. Rodriguez could help her students by creating a study guide for their work in which she helps them to ask:

  • How do I ask: How do I need to phrase my questions to an AI tool? How many questions should I ask and how should I ask them?
  • How do I know: …that the response is accurate, complete and valid?
  • How do I apply: …what I’m learning and use it to accomplish the goals for my lesson?
  • How do I change: …change my approach if I get stuck?

By gaining a basic understanding of GenAI literacy concepts and addressing common critical thinking strategies, Ms. Rodriguez can confidently explore and adopt AI tools to enhance her teaching and student learning about complex public health topics such as SARS CoV-2, implications for current disease outbreaks and strategies for health communication. These GenAI literacy lesson plan components are designed to work within many types of lessons and as such are meant to be integrated into different lesson plans. This lesson plan is designed to be adapted to work with ChatGPT
